What do organ and the exponential back-off in distributed systems have in common?


The music interval called the octave is the doubling/ halving of frequency depending on if you want a higher or lower pitch. The lowest A on a piano is typically 27.5 Hz and is the leftmost key on the keyboard. The organ can go as low as 16 Hz with the 32-foot or 9.75 meters pipe for a C0 using the scientific pitch or Verdi tuning. A C4 would be a nice 16 Hz * 2 * 2 * 2 * 2 = 256 Hz and a much more manageable 2-feet or 66 cm long. It would sound a little flat to our ears, because a concert C4 is supposed to be 261.63 Hz.


It turns out, to prevent the thundering herd problem and in general behave more gracefully, distributed systems tend to take a progressively longer pause between retries on failure. If you add a bit of jitter in the timing and a truncation or capping of the longest delay you can reasonably prevent many clients retrying at once and tipping over the system, timing out again or waiting seemingly indefinitely. The pause gives the server some time to process other inputs as the whole system cooperates basically agreeing on a form of back pressure.


For user interfaces the exponential back-off for binary exponents 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 seconds delay is cumbersome because the delay grows quickly and nobody likes waiting. The Fibonacci series 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 is a bit leaner. Of course, you can adjust the base of the delay to something else than a single second, e.g. 600 milliseconds and use the elements in the series as multipliers.


In some cases, you want to introduce delay exponentially at first reducing the pressure quickly and then switching to linear or constant retries or even stopping altogether. This can prevent very long delays, or in the case of battery powered devices or long outages it saves resources. It is a good thing to then retry on some trigger, e.g. reconnecting to a network such as Wi-Fi, changing of the adapter state such as when the network switch the physical server is connected to toggles the switch port or e.g. reboots. This behavior is useful when you didn't get a DHCP lease during the first series of tries.
